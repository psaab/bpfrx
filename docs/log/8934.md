## Narrow, and why narrow is the ceiling rather than a compromise

The obvious fix is to enumerate FRR's accepted community literals and reject
anything else. That is **a claim about another program's grammar that nothing
in this repo can verify** — get it wrong and it false-rejects working configs,
which is worse than the defect. The original author declined to enumerate for
exactly that reason and that reasoning still holds.

So the gate rejects **characters**, not names: a NON-REGEX member containing
anything outside `[A-Za-z0-9:_-]`. Every literal FRR accepts — `ASN:VALUE`,
large-community `A:B:C`, the well-known names — is spelled from that set, so a
member outside it is not one of them. Strictly weaker than an enumeration,
supportable from here, and it leaves the enumeration available to whoever has
FRR's grammar in hand.

**Limited to non-regex members on purpose.** In an `expanded` list the same `@`
is an ordinary literal inside a POSIX ERE and regcomp accepts it; rejecting it
there would false-reject something FRR takes.

## Two decisions I did NOT take over

Control characters and space are excluded from the check. They already have
dedicated handling — the #1798 strict gate rejects control characters, and
`sanitizeFRRValue` collapses them at render time so a leniently-loaded value
cannot inject an frr.conf line (#4097).

Claiming them here would not add protection, it would **replace** that
behaviour: an omitted definition instead of a sanitized rendered one. #4097
asserts the sanitized line and went red when I first over-reached. Taking a
decision another gate already made — in a change whose whole argument is
narrowness — is the wrong trade even when the outcome is defensible.

## The upgrade path, measured rather than reasoned about

`members "@evil"` is accepted at commit today, so a persisted config may already
carry one. If the reject fired on LOAD it would turn a running-but-broken node
into a non-starting one.

Measured on the function `Store.Load` actually calls:

| path | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| `CheckText` (strict commit) | accepted | **REFUSED**, naming the character |
| `compileTreeLenient` (Load, rollback) | accepted silently | **accepted WITH a warning** |

The #1960 no-brick split is what makes the fix safe, and the guard pins **both**
halves — the fix is only safe while they stay different. Booting through is
correct; booting through silently is not, so the warning is asserted too.

## Bound at the wiring, not the predicate

A unit test on `ValidCommunityMember` would pass while the render path stayed
reachable another way. The defect was demonstrated end to end, so that is where
it is asserted: text → strict commit → rendered frr.conf line.

This also turns the shared-predicate finding to advantage. The belt could not
catch the bug because it shares `ValidCommunityMember` with the gate
(*"shared so the two cannot drift"* — shared means they cannot disagree, **and**
that they cannot cover for each other). Extending the shared predicate makes
**both** ends hold at once.

The wiring cell asserts a legitimate member still renders **first** — otherwise
"nothing reached frr.conf" is satisfied by rendering nothing at all.

## Two opposite rulings on `@`, pinned together

    #8924  an SSH algorithm name MAY contain `@`   (aes256-gcm@openssh.com)
    #8934  a community LITERAL may NOT contain `@` (@evil)

Both correct, different grammars, and nothing pinned them apart. The likely
failure was never a regression but a well-meant **correction**: someone finds
one ruling, reads it as the repo's position on `@`, and "fixes" the other.
`TestAtSignRulingsAreOppositeOnPurpose8934` reds if either half is changed to
agree with the other, and asserts the asymmetry is per-SURFACE by keeping `@`
legal inside a community regex.
